# Professional Norwegian business writing

All output produced under this skill is **Norwegian bokmål**. These instructions
are in English; the text you write is not.

> **Organization-specific skills win.** If a workplace plugin provides a house
> style skill for the text type at hand (for example one for customer-facing
> Norwegian, or an English sibling for tender text), load
> that and treat this skill as the general baseline underneath it.

## Voice

Write modern, clear business Norwegian along klarspråk principles:

- Lead with the point. The reader should know what this is about, and what is
  expected of them, in the first two sentences.
- Active voice and finite verbs. Prefer *vi anbefaler* over *det anbefales*.
- Short sentences. Split anything that needs a second reading.
- No filler openings (*Jeg håper denne e-posten finner deg vel*), no throat
  clearing, no apologising for writing.
- Avoid anglicism-heavy consultant jargon (*leverage*, *touch base*, *på et
  high level*, *aligne*). Say it in Norwegian.

**Keep established English domain terms** where translating would reduce
precision — *cloud*, *pipeline*, *data lakehouse*, *machine learning*,
*deployment*, *stack*, product names, role titles used as-is in the industry.
Translate only when a natural Norwegian term is genuinely in common professional
use (*sikkerhet*, *arkitektur*, *krav*, *leveranse*, *tilbud*).

## Tone calibration

| Context | Register |
|---|---|
| Internal Teams / chat | Direct, first name, short paragraphs, contractions fine |
| Internal email / memo | Professional-neutral, structured, still concise |
| Customer-facing email | Polite but not deferential, concrete commitments and dates |
| Tender / formal proposal | Formal, precise, requirement-anchored, no marketing adjectives |
| Decision material to management | Recommendation first, then rationale, then consequences |

When the audience or formality level is unclear, ask — or default to
professional-neutral and say which register you assumed.

## Structures

**Email**: subject line that states the matter, not the topic (*Beslutning om
migrering innen 20. mars*, not *Migrering*) → one-line context → the ask or the
point → details or bullets → clear next step with owner and date → sign-off
(*Med vennlig hilsen* formal, *Hilsen* normal).

**Teams message**: the point in the first line. Details after. Bold the decision
or deadline. No greeting ritual on an ongoing thread.

**Meeting summary**: *Deltakere* → *Beslutninger* (each with owner) → *Oppgaver*
(task, owner, deadline) → *Åpne spørsmål* → *Neste møte*. Decisions and tasks
before discussion notes; most readers stop after the first two sections.

**Decision memo**: *Bakgrunn* → *Alternativer* → *Vurdering* → *Anbefaling* →
*Konsekvenser* (cost, risk, resources) → *Beslutning som bes tatt*.

## Common pitfalls

- **Capitalisation differs from English**: job titles, weekdays, months,
  languages, and nationalities are lowercase in Norwegian (*fredag*, *norsk*,
  *avdelingsleder*). Only proper nouns and sentence starts are capitalised.
- **Compound words are written together** in Norwegian (*prosjektleder*,
  *kundemøte*, *datakvalitet*). Særskriving is the most visible error there is.
- **Comma before subordinate clauses** introduced by *som*, *at*, *fordi* only
  when the clause is non-restrictive; always a comma before *men* joining two
  main clauses.
- **en/et consistency**: check gender agreement across the whole document,
  especially after editing (*et tilbud … dette tilbudet*, *en leveranse … denne
  leveransen*).
- **Consistent terminology**: pick one term per concept and keep it. Do not
  alternate between *kunde* and *oppdragsgiver* in the same document.
- **Numbers and dates**: Norwegian format — *20. mars 2026*, *1 250 000 kroner*,
  decimal comma (*3,5 millioner*).
- **Dashes**: use – (en dash) for ranges and parentheticals, not --.

## Working method

1. Identify audience, purpose, and the action you want from the reader.
2. Draft the structure before the prose.
3. Write, then cut 20%. First drafts of business Norwegian are almost always
   padded.
4. Read the opening aloud. If it does not say what this is and what is asked,
   rewrite it.
